You have an idea you'd normally park for "someday": a side project, an internal tool, a feature nobody has time for. Then you sit down with an AI model and the tools around it, and by the end of the weekend it works.

I want to be that engineer. This series is how I'm getting there, one build at a time.

First, what is "AI engineering"?

I used to think AI engineering meant training models: maths, GPUs and research papers. For most AI engineering roles, it doesn't.

AI engineering is building reliable products on top of models someone else trained.

When I read that, my first thought was: so it's backend work with a new dependency. It partly is. The catch is the dependency itself. It doesn't always give the same answer twice, it's sometimes confidently wrong, and you talk to it in English.

So the job isn't calling an API. It's building a system that works even when its most important component is unpredictable. As backend engineers, we've been doing a version of that for our whole careers.

Meet Design Dojo

Design Dojo is a mock system design interview app. You practise an interview with an AI and get an honest grade at the end. I chose it because I prepare for these interviews myself, so I'm the first user, and I can tell straight away when the AI does a bad job.

  • Alex, the interviewer, scopes the problem with you, watches your whiteboard, pushes back on hand-waving, then grades you on storage, distributed systems, observability and problem scoping.
  • Sam, the architect, writes a model solution after the interview, compares it with yours and answers follow-up questions.

It started as a single HTML file with the prompts written inline. That prototype taught me my first big lesson.

How a mock-interview app makes me an AI engineer

Fair question. Here's what it has forced me to work out.

1. It's a pipeline, not a chatbot

One interview is several model calls, each with one job. Deciding what each call is allowed to see turned out to be the real design work.

2. It has to keep a secret

When an interview starts, Alex writes a hidden spec: the real requirements, the scale and the curveballs. The candidate must not see it until the debrief. I learned quickly that "please don't reveal this" in a prompt is not a security boundary, so the spec stays on the server.

3. It has to avoid bias

Sam designs the model solution without seeing your design. If it could see yours, it would quietly anchor to it. Comparing the two is a separate step. Here's who sees what:

4. It has to "see" things that aren't text

A model can't look at a whiteboard the way you do, so I turn the diagram into structured text, with an id for every box and arrow. That also lets Alex pin a comment to one specific box.

5. It has to trade speed against quality

A live interview turn has to come back quickly. A debrief can take a minute if it's thorough. So each call gets its own effort level.

6. It has to grade fairly

A wrong grade is worse than no grade. This is pushing me towards evals, the part of AI engineering I know least about so far. There's a post coming on it.
